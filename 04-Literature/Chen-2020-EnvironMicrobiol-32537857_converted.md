## Introduction

Ras proteins are low-molecular-weight GTPases, which

localize to the plasma membrane (PM) and play key roles

in regulating various cellular processes, including cell

growth, differentiation, division and survival (Weeks and

Spiegelman, 2003; Wennerberg et al., 2005). Ras pro-

teins are binary molecular switches that cycle between

active [guanosine triphosphate (GTP)-bound] and inac-

tive [guanosine diphosphate (GDP)-bound] conformations

mediated

guanine

nucleotide

exchange

factors

(GEFs) and GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs). GEF

proteins promote the release of GDP from stable, inactive

GDP-bound Ras and the binding of GTP to the GDP-free

form of Ras, whereas GAPs inactivate GTPases by

hydrolyzing GTP to GDP (Vetter and Wittinghofer, 2001;

Wennerberg et al., 2005). In Saccharomyces cerevisiae,

two Ras proteins, Ras1 and Ras2, were positively con-

trolled by two GEFs, Cdc25 and Sdc25 and negatively

regulated by two GAPs, Ira1 and Ira2 (Broek et al., 1987;

Tanaka et al., 1990). After activation by GEFs, Ras pro-

teins bind to and activate adenylate cyclase Cyr1, which

is required for the synthesis of intracellular cyclic adeno-

sine monophosphate (cAMP), thus activating the cAMP-

dependent protein kinase A (PKA) (Broek et al., 1985;

Toda et al., 1985). Active PKA phosphorylates several

proteins associated with cell cycle progression, energy

metabolism and transcription (Thevelein et al., 2000;

Muller et al., 2003). The so-called Ras/cAMP/PKA path-

way is known to be involved in numerous cellular activi-

ties and regulate stress responses in different species

(Thevelein, 1994; Thevelein et al., 2000). Proteins in the

Ras/cAMP/PKA pathway are associated with various

human diseases, including cancer, RASopathies, capil-

lary malformations, psychiatric and neurodevelopmental

disorders (Simanshu et al., 2017). In several pathogenic

fungi, the Ras/cAMP/PKA signalling pathway is critical for

Fusarium

causes Fusarium head blight (FHB) in cereal crops world-

wide (Goswami and Kistler, 2004; Starkey et al., 2007).

FHB can result in severe yield losses. For example,

severe FHB epidemics have occurred in China during the

last two decades and led to annual yield losses of more

than 3.41 million tons (Chen et al., 2019). In addition,

pose enormous threat to both humans and animals

Due to the lack of resistant plant cultivars and occurrence

of fungicide-resistance isolates, effective management of

FHB and its associated mycotoxins remains extremely

difﬁcult (Beyer et al., 2006; Paul et al., 2008). Compre-

hensive understanding the molecular mechanisms of

pathogenicity and mycotoxin biosynthesis might facilitate

the development of effective measures for controlling this

devastating disease.

It has been shown that the Ras/cAMP/PKA pathway

plays a critical role in the pathogenicity of F. graminearum.

Two Ras proteins, Ras1 and Ras2, were identiﬁed in this

fungus. Ras2 was shown to be important for virulence,

whereas mutation of RAS1 was lethal (Bluhm et al., 2007).

Mutants of the PKA catalytic subunit gene CPK1 or the

adenylate cyclase gene FAC1 signiﬁcantly reduced the vir-

ulence of the pathogen and the production of DON in cul-

tures or infected plant tissues (Bormann et al., 2014; Hu

et al., 2014; Li et al., 2018). To date, RasGEFs in the

Ras/cAMP/PKA pathway have not been identiﬁed, and

their functions in cross-talk between the Ras/cAMP/PKA

and MAP kinase signalling pathways remain unknown in

F. graminearum. In this study, we characterized the Ras

GTPase

FgRas2-interacting

RasGEF,

FgCdc25,

exhibited similar defects as those of the ΔFgras2 in vege-

tative growth, sexual reproduction, stress response and

virulence. In addition, FgCdc25 regulated DON biosynthe-

sis through the cAMP signalling pathway. Furthermore,

FgCdc25 mediated cross-talk between the Ras/cAMP/

PKA pathway and the MAP Kinase cascades by interacting

with MAP kinase FgSte11 and FgBck1, which regulates

the fungal penetration and cell wall integrity respectively.

FgCdc25 is required for virulence in F. graminearum

To determine the role of FgCdc25 in virulence, we ﬁrst

evaluated the virulence of the ΔFgcdc25 on ﬂowering

wheat heads. The wild-type strain and complemented

strains caused typical scab symptoms in the inoculated

and nearby spikelets after 14 days post-inoculation (dpi).

The ΔFgras2 was able to colonize in the inoculated spike-

let, but failed to spread to nearby spikelets (Fig. 3A). No

visible disease was developed for the ΔFgcdc25 on inoc-

ulated spikelet under the same condition (Fig. 3A). To fur-

ther conﬁrm these results, leaves of wheat seedlings were

inoculated with mycelial plugs of the deletion mutants and

wild-type strain, and similar results were obtained as those

on ﬂowering wheat heads (Fig. 3B). To rule out the viru-

lence defect in mutants is host-dependent, we also inocu-

lated silk tissue with different strains. As shown in Fig. 3C,

the wild-type strain was able to colonize silk tissue and

grow down silk channels. However, mutants showed no

visible symptom when compared to PH-1. The defect in

virulence of the mutants was fully recovered to the wild-

type level in the corresponding complementation strains.

These results suggest that FgCdc25 is essential for viru-

lence in F. graminearum.

## Discussion

Ras protein signal transduction is tightly regulated in

fungi. In S. cerevisiae, the activation of the small

GTPases Ras1 and Ras2 are positively modulated by

two RasGEFs, Cdc25 and Sdc25 and negatively regu-

lated by GTPase activating proteins (Tanaka et al., 1990;

Jones et al., 1991). Cdc25 was originally identiﬁed as an

essential protein to regulate Ras1/2p protein activity (Lai

et al., 1993). However, subsequent studies suggested

that CDC25 is dispensable in genetic backgrounds that

encode a functional Cdc25 paralog, Sdc25 (Folch-Mallol

et al., 2004). Unlike S. cerevisiae, phylogenetic analysis

indicated that only one ortholog of Cdc25 and Sdc25,

FgCdc25, exists in F. graminearum as well as in all

tested fungi in this study (Fig. S1). More strikingly, the

ΔFgcdc25 was not lethal, despite only one Cdc25-like

RasGEF

ortholog

exists

graminearum.

ΔFgcdc25 exhibited radial and hyphal growth defects,

similar to those in the ΔFgras2 (Fig. 2). Consistently,

Cdc25-like

RasGEFs

fumigatus

C. higginsianum are also dispensable, but are critical for

mycelial growth. However, the conserved function of

Cdc25-like RasGEFs in regulating vegetative growth in

fungi might not be through the cAMP signalling, because

exogenous cAMP cannot rescue the growth defects of

ΔFgcdc25 (Fig. S3).

At the upstream of the Ras/cAMP/PKA pathway,

Cdc25 interacted with Ras1/2 and was mainly localized

to plasma membrane, but was also localized to nuclear

and endoplasmic reticulum membrane in S. cerevisiae

(Tisi

al.,

2008;

Dong

Bai,

2011;

Belotti

et al., 2012). In Colletotrichum orbiculare, ﬂuorescent sig-

nals of RFP-CoRas2 fusion protein were distributed

throughout the entire cell and mainly localized in the cyto-

plasm (Harata and Kubo, 2014). In this study, we found

that FgCdc25-GFP was mainly dispersed throughout the

cytoplasm and also exhibited punctate ﬂuorescence pat-

terns (Fig. 7A). FgRas2, but not FgRas1, directly inter-

acted with FgCdc25 and was also located in cytoplasm

and punctate structures (Fig. 1). The different localization

patterns

other

GTPase

ple, Ypt51, a Rab family GTPase in S. cerevisiae, was

reported to localize in plasma membrane and endo-

somes, whereas its ortholog in F. graminearum was only

located in vacuole membrane (Zheng et al., 2015). In

addition, function of structural domains of FgCdc25 was

also different from those in Cdc25 of S. cerevisiae. Previ-

ous studies showed that the SH3 domain regulated

Cdc25

stability

protein–protein

interactions

with

adenylyl cyclase in S. cerevisiae, thus facilitating Ras

regulation of cAMP signalling (Van Aelst et al., 1990;

Freeman et al., 1996; Mintzer and Field, 1999). However,

the SH3 domain truncated mutant FgCdc25ΔSH3-GFP

exhibited similar phenotypes as those in the wild type on

protein localization, growth, virulence and its interaction

with FgRas2. In contrast, the RasGEF_N and RasGEF

domains are required for localization and function of

FgCdc25, which is consistent with previous studies in

yeast (Van Aelst et al., 1990; Freeman et al., 1996;

Mintzer and Field, 1999). Taken together, Cdc25-like

RasGEFs show variable localization patterns, interaction

manners with Ras proteins and biological function among

different fungal species.

The Ras/cAMP/PKA pathway and the MAPK cascades

on host plants in the fungal pathogen F. graminearum.

FgCdc25 is involved in DON biosynthesis via the cAMP

signal pathway. More importantly, FgCdc25 also partici-

cade to negatively control cell wall integrity (Fig. 8). Fur-

ther investigation on the function of FgCdc25 and its

regulation may provide a novel target to combat FHB and

possibly other plant-pathogenic fungi.
